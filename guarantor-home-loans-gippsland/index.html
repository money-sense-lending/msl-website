How a guarantor home loan actually works

The deposit is what stops most first home buyers, not the repayments. Plenty of people around the Valley are comfortably paying more in rent than the loan would cost them — they just can’t get 20% together while they’re doing it.

A guarantor home loan is the arrangement that most often solves that. A family member — usually a parent — offers a slice of the equity in their own property as additional security for your loan. Not cash. They don’t hand you money, they don’t go on your title, and they don’t make your repayments.

The lender ends up holding two properties as security instead of one, so its overall exposure is lower. Which means you can often borrow without a full 20% deposit, and frequently without lenders mortgage insurance.

What it changes

The two things it usually moves are the deposit hurdle and the LMI bill. LMI is a one-off insurance premium that protects the lender, not you, and it’s typically charged when you borrow above 80% of the property value. On a Gippsland purchase it can run into thousands. A guarantee that lifts the combined security above that threshold can remove it.

Limit the guarantee — this is the part people get wrong

A guarantee should be limited: capped at a specific dollar amount, not open-ended across your whole loan. A limited guarantee means the guarantor is only ever exposed to that capped portion — typically the shortfall between your deposit and what the lender wants — rather than the full debt.

Most lenders will do this, but not all structure it the same way, and the cap doesn’t always default to the smallest sensible figure. It’s worth being deliberate about, because it’s the single number that defines your parents’ risk for the next few years.

What a guarantor is genuinely taking on

This is the conversation worth having properly at the kitchen table, not glossed over. A guarantor is legally responsible for the guaranteed portion of the loan. If the loan defaults and the shortfall can’t be covered, the lender can pursue that security — and in the worst case that means their home.

  • It affects their own borrowing. While the guarantee is live, lenders count it when assessing what your parents can borrow. If they’re thinking about their own move, an investment, or a caravan, that matters.
  • They’ll need their own legal advice. Lenders generally require a guarantor to get their own legal advice before signing, and to sign the guarantee separately. That’s a protection, not a formality.
  • It usually needs to be family. Most lenders restrict guarantors to immediate family, and many want them to be Australian citizens or permanent residents with enough usable equity.
  • Retirees can be harder. Some lenders are cautious about guarantors who are retired or nearing it. Others will consider it. Policy varies more here than almost anywhere else.

Getting the guarantee released

A guarantee isn’t meant to be permanent, and that’s the part families rarely ask about upfront. Once your loan balance has fallen far enough relative to your property’s value — through repayments, growth, or both — you can generally apply to have the guarantee removed and your parents’ property released as security.

It isn’t automatic. You apply, the lender revalues, and the guarantee comes off if the numbers stack up. Worth knowing at the start: the faster you get the loan down, the sooner your parents are out of it. Plenty of people set the loan up with that as the explicit goal.

What this looks like around Gippsland

It’s a common shape here. Parents who bought in Traralgon, Morwell or Moe years ago are often sitting on equity they never think about, while their kids are stuck renting locally and watching the deposit target move. A guarantee bridges that without anyone selling anything or handing over savings.

Dougal bought his first property at around 20 or 21 on apprentice wages, and the loan was structured badly from the start — something he only worked out years later, once it had cost him. So the structure conversation here isn’t abstract. Getting a guarantee capped properly and set up with a release in mind is the difference between a good arrangement and one that quietly causes problems.

Before you raise it with your parents

  1. Find out whether you actually need one. Some buyers who assume they need a guarantor don’t — there are other paths, including government-backed schemes for eligible first home buyers (eligibility applies). Start with the numbers.
  2. Work out the gap. Knowing the specific dollar figure you’re asking them to secure makes it a real conversation instead of an open-ended one.
  3. Check where your parents are at. If they have plans of their own in the next few years, that shapes the answer.
  4. Compare lenders before applying. Guarantor policy differs a lot — who qualifies, how the guarantee is capped, and what’s needed to release it later.

Guarantor home loan questions, answered

Does a guarantor have to give me money?

No. A guarantor offers equity in their own property as additional security for your loan. No cash changes hands, they don’t go on your title, and they don’t make your repayments. The lender simply holds a second property as backup security.

What happens if I can’t make the repayments?

The guarantor is legally responsible for the guaranteed portion of the loan. If the loan defaults and there is a shortfall, the lender can pursue that security — and in the worst case that can mean their home. It is the reason the guarantee should be capped at a specific amount and why lenders require guarantors to get their own legal advice first.

Can the guarantee be removed later?

Generally yes. Once your loan balance has fallen far enough relative to your property’s value — through repayments, growth, or both — you can apply to have the guarantee released and your guarantor’s property discharged as security. It isn’t automatic: the lender reassesses and usually revalues. Many people set the loan up with that release as the goal from day one.

Does being a guarantor affect my parents’ own borrowing?

Yes, while it is in place. Lenders take the guarantee into account when assessing what a guarantor can borrow themselves. If your parents are thinking about moving, buying an investment or borrowing for anything else in the next few years, that is worth raising before the guarantee is signed rather than after.

Who is allowed to be a guarantor?

Most lenders restrict it to immediate family — usually parents, sometimes siblings or grandparents. They generally need to own property with enough usable equity and be in a stable financial position. Policy varies, particularly around retired guarantors, which is one reason the lender you choose matters here.

Do I still need a deposit if I have a guarantor?

Usually some, yes. Most lenders still want to see a genuine savings contribution from you, and you will still need to cover purchase costs. A guarantee is generally used to bridge the gap rather than replace the deposit entirely. How much you need depends on the lender and your situation.

Getting a Loan with Money Sense Lending

1. Understanding Your Needs

Your mortgage journey begins with a detailed consultation with your Finance & Mortgage Broker. Your broker will listen to your property goals, whether you’re buying a home, investing in real estate, or seeking a commercial loan. They will assess your financial situation, considering factors like income, savings, liabilities, and credit history, to provide tailored advice on loan options available to you.

2. Financial Positioning

The next step involves your broker conducting a thorough evaluation of your financial position. This includes reviewing your income, bank statements, assets, liabilities, credit history, and savings. Your broker will then calculate your borrowing capacity, helping you understand how much you can afford to borrow and how much your monthly repayments might be. Your broker will also assess whether you qualify for any interest rate discounts or government schemes, like first-home buyer grants

3. Comparing Loan Options

Once your financial situation is fully assessed, your broker will begin comparing loan products from a range of lenders across Australia. They will help you weigh the benefits of fixed interest rate loans versus variable interest rate loans, as well as exploring options for offset accounts and interest rate discounts. They will also explain important terms like loan features, fees, and other considerations like the potential for future rate changes or LVR adjustments.

4. Pre-Approval Process

Pre-approval is a crucial step in the property buying process. With pre-approval, you’ll have a clear idea of the loan amount you can borrow, which strengthens your position in the local property market. Your broker will help you gather the necessary documents and submit them to the lender for pre-approval, ensuring the process is as quick and straightforward as possible.

5. Submitting the Loan Application

Once pre-approval is in hand, your broker will help you complete the loan application. They will guide you through submitting all necessary paperwork to the lender, including documents like bank statements, proof of income, and details of existing liabilities. Your broker will communicate directly with the lender to ensure the application is processed quickly, ensuring no delays in getting you the loan you need.

6. Loan Approval & Settlement

After your loan is approved, your broker will review the final loan offer with you. They will also work with you to ensure you understand the terms of the loan and guide you through the settlement process. Your broker will remain available to help you navigate any final hurdles before you complete the purchase of your property.

7. Finalising Ownership

The final step is settlement, when your loan is officially advanced, and you take ownership of the property. Your broker will coordinate with the lender and conveyancer to ensure the transfer goes smoothly. Once the settlement is complete, you’ll officially become the owner of the property, and your Finance & Mortgage Broker will continue to offer guidance to help you manage your loan and stay on track with repayments.
